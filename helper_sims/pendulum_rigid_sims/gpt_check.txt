Born Temüjin around 1162 on the windswept steppes of present-day Mongolia, Genghis Khan rose from a perilous childhood—marked by clan exile, capture, and daring escapes—to forge strategic alliances and impose a strict meritocratic discipline that united the fractious Mongol tribes under his banner by 1206, when he was proclaimed “Chinggis Khaan.” Over the next two decades he engineered a series of lightning‐fast campaigns—against the Xi Xia and Jin dynasties in North China, the Khwārazmian Empire across Central Asia, and kingdoms as far as the Caucasus and the Kievan Rus’—that combined unparalleled mobility, psychological warfare, and a codified legal system (the Yassa) to create the largest contiguous land empire in history. His rule fostered trans-Eurasian trade, diplomatic exchange, and unprecedented cultural diffusion along what would become the revitalized Silk Roads, even as his armies wrought devastation on cities that resisted. At his death in 1227, possibly from injuries sustained in battle against the Tangut Xi Xia, he was buried in an unmarked grave on the sacred Burkhan Khaldun, leaving a meticulously organized imperial administration and succession plan that enabled his descendants—most notably Ögödei, Möngke, and Kublai—to extend Mongol dominion from Korea to the Danube and from Siberia to the shores of the Persian Gulf, indelibly reshaping the medieval world.
